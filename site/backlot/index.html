    00Overview

    Backlot, a CMS for streaming services

    From delivery to release, one region at a time

    Streaming teams use Backlot to take films and series from delivery to release, region by region. I designed the whole workflow for both sides: dashboards, upload and file matching, fields that fill from the file, the publisher’s review and artwork tests.

    01The problem

    The work is not hard.The loop is.

    The problem

    For the team: getting one episode ready for one region means matching its files, filling about forty fields and passing a publisher’s review. Much of it is typing what the file already says, and mistakes only surface at review, where each one costs a round.

    For the service: 86% of streaming operators in a 2026 survey said poor metadata costs them real money, and a wrong value for one region can put a title on air where its rights don’t allow it.

    Finding

    Files arrive broken

    Deliveries come with missing, extra or mislabelled files, and each one has to be matched to its episode by hand.

    Finding

    Almost every field changes by region

    Title, synopsis, age rating, release date, rights, audio and subtitles each need their own version per region.

    Finding

    Mistakes hide in the structure

    Metadata entered on the wrong level, a season instead of an episode, only shows up at review.

    Finding

    Feedback doesn’t point at a field

    Most tools let a reviewer comment, but a rejection is still a note. The editor has to work out which field it means.

    North star

    Release-ready sooner, with less typing

    Every decision on this page answers one question: does it get an episode release-ready sooner, with less typing and fewer rounds of review?

    The bet

    My bet: most of the delay is retyping and rework. In my design of Episode 2, the editor types 5 of its 41 fields; the file, the delivery and the season fill the rest. If feedback also points at fields, time to release-ready drops. I haven’t tested it with an editor yet.

    Research

    Eight tools, each solving part of the job

    Streaming CMSs are built in-house and rarely shown, so I studied the tools that cover pieces of the workflow: headless CMSs, a video platform, creative review tools, and GitHub’s pull request review, where a reviewer comments on an exact line and the author resolves each comment.

    Dashboards

    Where each role starts the day.

    1. Blockers, counts and the work the editor left off, on one page.
    2. What airs soon, what waits for review, and what each region has received.
    3. The editor’s counts start with Rejected, because that is the work that came back to them. The publisher’s start with Pending review: each role sees first what needs them.
    4. The editor’s banner names files that couldn’t be assigned. The publisher’s names the one item that airs within seven days and still needs a decision.
    5. Missing fields grouped across everything assigned to the editor, so one pass fills the Spanish synopsis for seven items.
    6. In place of blockers, the publisher sees each region’s deliveries this week and how far along they are.

    Workspaces

    Where the work itself happens.

    1. The editor’s page is called Workspace: everything they are working on, sorted by status, with rejections underneath.
    2. The publisher’s page is called Reviews: only what reaches them, with every past decision underneath.
    3. The editor’s list runs through all eight statuses, from To do to Published.
    4. To do and In progress are the editor’s, so the publisher’s list leaves them out and starts at Pending review. Scheduled stays: the publisher decides what goes live.
    5. The publisher’s queue is sorted by what airs first and shows how long each item has waited. In the same place, the editor’s table shows Blockers.
    6. The editor sees rejected work as cards, so each reason shows before the item opens.
    7. The publisher keeps a record of every decision: who decided, when, and when it was released.
    03Features

    Features

    Eight decisions across the workflow

    1. 01Treating region as a structural scope, not just a filter
    2. 02The editor only fills what is missing
    3. 03Designed for the delivery that arrives broken
    4. 04A rejection is a list of reasons
    5. 05AI suggests, the editor accepts
    6. 06Artwork is chosen by taste, not by country
    7. 07Each row offers only its next step
    8. 08Once review starts, content is locked

    Feature 01

    Treating region as a structural scope, not just a filter

    Title, synopsis, age rating, dates, rights, audio and subtitles all change by region. The editor picks a region first, and the whole page answers for it.

    Every level

    Series, season, episode and file each keep their own fields and status, so the editor can tell which level a change affects. Age rating rolls up: a season takes the highest rating among its episodes in that region.

    Feature 02

    The editor only fills what is missing

    Runtime, codec and audio are read from the file, and the delivery fills most of the rest. What is still missing sits at the top of the page.

    1. Every empty field is grouped in “Needs your input”, above what the upload already filled.
    2. Values from the delivery are locked. Each field unlocks on its own, so every change is deliberate.
    3. Submit hands the episode to the publisher. Save keeps it under In progress, ready to pick up later.
    4. “4 of 8” tells the editor how much is left and where to start. “Incomplete” would only say something is wrong.
    5. The season page lists what must be complete before the season can be scheduled.

    A season keeps its own metadata, apart from its episodes

    Episodes, season metadata, season assets, deliveries per region and the audit log, with the readiness panel in view on every tab.

    Feature 03

    Designed for the delivery that arrives broken

    unhappy path

    Deliveries often arrive incomplete or mislabelled. The design starts from that case, so a broken delivery never blocks the files that are fine.

    1. Processing reads each file’s technical metadata and links it to its episode. Files it can’t place go to Needs assignment.
    2. The upload folds into a panel in the corner, so the editor can open other pages and come back to it.
    3. The drop zone stays open, so files can be added while others are still uploading.
    4. Uploading, Processing, Ready, Needs assignment and Failed belong to the file, apart from the episode’s workflow status.

    Resolving the files processing couldn’t place

    Each problem gets its own fix: assign files in bulk, keep or replace a duplicate master, or create the episode a file points to.

    Feature 04

    A rejection is a list of reasons

    The publisher approves an item, or sends it back with reasons the editor can act on one by one. Each reason is pinned to its field.

    01

    Comments become reasons

    The publisher comments on the metadata row itself. Each comment becomes a reason with its field and a category.

    02

    Fixed in place

    A flagged row stays marked on the page with the publisher’s note, so the editor fixes it in place.

    03

    Counted before it is sent

    Before anything is sent, Reject shows how many reasons there are and who gets the item back.

    When the same reason comes back

    A clear note can still fail. If one reason returns on three episodes of a season, the editor isn’t the problem: the wrong value is coming from upstream. Because every reason carries a field and a category, a repeat can be counted and fixed where it starts: the season, the delivery spec or an automatic check.

    Try it

    Flag two fields as the publisher, then fix them as the editor.

    Feature 05

    AI suggests, the editor accepts

    The AI points at likely rejections before the publisher sees them and proposes values like keywords. Nothing is written until the editor accepts.

    01

    Built on past rejections

    Suggestions come from past rejection reasons. Each reason carries a field and a category, so they can be counted per team and per region.

    02

    Rules and AI stay apart

    Automatic checks pass or fail by rule. The AI sits apart and only raises judgement calls, saying how sure it is.

    03

    Nothing written on its own

    Accept writes the value, Dismiss asks for a reason. Either way, the decision stays in the activity log.

    Rules and AI, side by side

    Rule checks pass or fail. AI suggestions name their evidence and how sure they are.

    Feature 06

    Artwork is chosen by taste, not by country

    Most viewing starts from a recommendation, so the thumbnail does much of the selling. Taste predicts a click better than age or location, so each season carries one thumbnail per taste cluster. Sources: Netflix on taste clusters, Fortune, Netflix on personalised artwork, Dezeen.

    01

    One thumbnail per taste

    Action, drama, romance and mystery fans each see the scene most likely to make them click.

    02

    A/B tested per region

    Variants are tested against each other in each region, with the time left on the test, so the editor knows when the artwork is final.

    03

    Backed by click-through

    Each variant shows its click-through rate, and the leader is marked.

    Feature 07

    Each row offers only its next step

    Review while an episode waits, Publish once it’s approved: each row shows only the action its status allows.

    Feature 08

    Once review starts, content is locked

    While the publisher reviews, every field is read-only. After approval, a change needs an edit request: it is meant for news from outside, like a new regional age rating, and it reopens one section at a time.

    04

    What I changed

    Why I moved from a single rejection modal to field-pinned reasons

    The first version

    The first version allowed one rejection reason per item, and I caught myself labelling rejected items “Multiple Issues”. That label said something was wrong without saying what, so reasons became a list, each pinned to a field and resolvable on its own.

    What I took out

    Most of what I added stayed. Four things didn’t.

    Taken out

    Availability as a ninth status

    On air, Ended and Expired showed up in work queues as if someone had to act on them. They became their own layer, shown only on headers and in the library.

    Cost: two sets of labels to learn.

    Taken out

    An AI score

    “72% likely to be rejected” gave the editor nothing to do. A named reason does.

    Cost: some flags read as less certain.

    Taken out

    Rejections first

    Opening the workspace on Rejections made a bad day the default view. Workflow leads now.

    Cost: rejection cards sit one scroll lower.

    Taken out

    Bulk unlock

    Unlocking every source field at once was fast, but lost which value changed and why. Fields unlock one at a time.

    Cost: multi-field corrections take longer.

    08

    What's next

    How I would measure it, and what comes next

    Measure

    Time from delivery to release-ready

    The north star, per region. Alongside it: rejection rounds per episode, fields typed by hand, and thumbnail click-through for viewers. The threshold I would watch: one reason returning on three episodes of a season.

    Test

    Test it with editors

    I would test two things first: do the rejection categories cover what publishers actually send back, and do editors trust the split between fields they fill and fields the upload already filled.

    Design

    Design what I scoped out

    Unpublishing, bulk metadata editing and notification routing are each a small product of their own, and each one touches the same loop.

    Later

    Let the system do more of the tagging

    Automatic thumbnail generation and ML-based tagging would feed the taste clusters, and a person would still confirm each one.
